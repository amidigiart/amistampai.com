Semantic Notarization

Certifying not just that something exists — but what it means and why it was created.

By Mihai Roșca · BRIDGRAI · September 2026

The Problem with Traditional Notarization

Traditional digital notarization answers one question: did this document exist at this point in time? A timestamp. A hash. A proof of existence.

But existence is the least interesting thing about a document. What matters is:

  • What does it mean? — the semantic content the author intended to communicate
  • Why was it created? — the intention behind the act of creation
  • Who stands behind it? — cryptographic authorship, not just possession

A photograph exists. But is it evidence, art, or propaganda? A contract exists. But was it signed under duress or with full understanding? A research paper exists. But was it written to advance knowledge or to game metrics?

Existence without meaning is noise.

What Semantic Notarization Certifies

Semantic notarization extends traditional timestamping into four verifiable layers:

1

Existence

SHA-256 content hash proves the document existed at a specific moment. Identical to traditional notarization.

2

Authorship

ECDSA P-256 digital signature binds the document to a specific cryptographic identity. The private key never leaves the author's device.

3

Meaning

The author declares what the document means — a human-readable semantic statement that becomes cryptographically bound to the content hash.

4

Intention

The author declares why the document was created — the purpose, the context, the motivation. This too is cryptographically bound and immutable.

All four layers are signed together in a single cryptographic operation and can be independently verified. Optionally, the combined proof is anchored on the Tezos blockchain for permanent, decentralized verification.

The Technical Stack

Semantic notarization as implemented in AmiStampAI uses open, verifiable standards:

  • SHA-256 — content hashing (Web Crypto API, runs locally)
  • ECDSA P-256 — digital signatures (NIST standard, keys generated and stored on device)
  • Tezos blockchain — optional anchoring for permanent, decentralized proof
  • JSON — stamp format is open and machine-readable

No vendor lock-in. No proprietary formats. No keys uploaded to servers. The stamp is self-contained and verifiable by anyone with access to the public key.

Use Cases

Intellectual Property

Prove not just that you created something, but what you intended it to mean. Prior art disputes become clearer when the semantic record exists alongside the timestamp.

AI-Generated Content

Declare whether content was human-authored, AI-assisted, or AI-generated. Semantic notarization provides a verifiable disclosure mechanism — not a label that can be stripped, but a cryptographic attestation.

Legal Documents

Contracts, agreements, and declarations carry more weight when the meaning and intention are cryptographically bound. "I understood what I was signing" becomes verifiable.

Research and Academia

Establish priority for ideas, not just papers. A semantic stamp on a research concept proves when you had the idea and what you meant by it — before peer review, before publication.

Journalism and Investigations

Certify the editorial intention behind a piece. "This is evidence" vs. "this is commentary" vs. "this is satire" — the declaration is immutable.

Why This Matters Now

Three forces converge in 2026:

  1. AI content flood — when anyone can generate anything, provenance is the only differentiator
  2. EU AI Act — mandates transparency about AI-generated content and automated decision-making
  3. Post-truth erosion — trust in institutions declines, trust in verifiable cryptographic proof does not

Traditional notarization proves you were there. Semantic notarization proves you meant it.
